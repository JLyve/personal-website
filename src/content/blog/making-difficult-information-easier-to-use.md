There's a person I think about whenever I'm working on content for Magnolia Cremations.

They may have just lost someone at 2 a.m. They're standing in a hospital hallway with a phone in one hand, trying to figure out what happens next.

When I started working with Magnolia near the end of 2024, there was no real system for creating educational content. The obvious missing piece was a blog, but the gap was bigger than that. There wasn't a regular way to research the questions people had, turn those answers into something useful, and make sure they could find that information through Google or the AI tools they were starting to use.

Magnolia is a regional cremation provider serving Indiana and Kentucky. I wanted our reach online to extend nationwide. I wanted Magnolia to become a place people could turn to for clear information about cremation and death care, even if they lived nowhere near either state.

I wanted someone to find one of our articles and come away thinking, "These people know what they're talking about, and they can explain it in a way I actually understand."

That was the real goal. The blog was where the work would live, but publishing articles was only the beginning. I had to build the system behind them.

Almost two years later, Magnolia has a growing library of articles and nearly four times as many clicks from Google Search. We also have a content process that keeps changing as I learn more.

I built that process while doing the work. Mostly, I figured it out as I went.
Magnolia helps people with cremation, death certificates, veterans benefits, Medicaid questions, Social Security questions, and other subjects that can affect a family's money, time, and legal responsibilities.

A wrong answer could create a real problem for someone who already has enough problems.

Many of the subjects we cover also fall into the kind of territory Google calls "[Your Money or Your Life](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content)" content. That's Google's term for information that can seriously affect someone's health, finances, safety, or well-being.

That set the bar for me. The articles had to be accurate, and anything important had to come from a source we could defend.

If a competitor, funeral director, attorney, or skeptical reader questioned something we published, I wanted to be able to show where the information came from. I didn't want our answer to be, "Well, that's what the AI said."

Magnolia's owners weren't going to write the articles. That was on me. I had to build the process and learn how to do the work well.

Researching and writing some of these articles entirely by hand could take weeks. The tools I use now let me do that work in days.

But faster only helps if the information stays trustworthy.
I can open an article I worked on six months ago and immediately start finding things I want to change.

The section headings aren't as clear as they should be. The formatting looks off. A statement needs a citation. One section needs more information, while another needs less.

Sometimes I look at an older article and think, "That's horrible."

It probably isn't horrible. My standards have changed.

The earliest articles had fewer facts and fewer links to original sources. I also steered around laws because I wasn't comfortable researching and explaining them yet.

Readers couldn't see Aaron Scott's role in the articles, either. That mattered, because I'm a marketer, not a funeral director. I can research and explain information, but I can't pretend to have professional experience I don't have.

[Aaron is Magnolia's vice president and a licensed funeral director in Kentucky and Indiana](https://magnoliacremations.com/pages/our-team). He also serves as the Clark County coroner.

Aaron reviews the articles for factual accuracy and gives the final approval before they go live. Without that connection, readers had fewer reasons to trust that someone with real death-care experience had checked the information.

Some of the early writing sounded more like AI, too.

There hasn't been one major failure that forced me to rethink everything. Overall, the process has gone fairly smoothly. It grew through hundreds of smaller moments when I noticed something that could be better.

I might read that trustworthy content should make the writer's experience and knowledge clear. Then I would go through our articles and figure out what needed to change.

Or I might learn that AI tools have an easier time using a page when the answer appears clearly near the top. That changed how we opened our articles.

A draft that sounded generic meant I needed better writing instructions. A statement without enough support meant I needed another check in the review process.

Sometimes the problem wasn't the writing at all. The reader needed a definition, a clearer answer, or some context we hadn't given them.

So I started thinking beyond individual articles. I built a glossary for death-care terms that most people don't hear every day. I started adding useful questions and answers to articles and pages throughout the site.

That became the pattern. I would learn something, look at what we were doing, find the gap, and add a step to fix it.
If I'm writing about something specific to Kentucky or Indiana, I may put together a list of questions for Aaron. He knows things that I don't and that no AI tool does.

Sometimes Aaron opens ChatGPT, answers my questions out loud, and sends me the transcript. That gives me his wording and his actual experience.

So when an article sounds like it came from a funeral director, that's because a funeral director gave me the answer.

If the subject is national, I may have Claude, ChatGPT, or both conduct deep research. Sometimes I ask them to compare what they found.

If they disagree, that's useful. It shows me where I need to keep digging.

If they agree, that still doesn't make the answer true.

For the claims that count, I go find the original law, government page, official program information, or another source that backs up what we're saying.

AI helps me do the research. It doesn't get to decide what's true.

Aaron can still end up in a national article. Sometimes Claude reaches a question during the research that it can't answer well. When that happens, it may suggest asking Aaron for his professional view.

I don't force every article through the exact same steps. The process changes based on the question, the risk of getting it wrong, and who is best qualified to answer it.
Sometimes I write the first draft myself. Sometimes Claude writes it using the detailed instructions I've built for Magnolia.

Those instructions cover company facts, terminology rules, and how the article should be structured. They also explain how to write in a voice that sounds like Aaron.

I built a separate set of instructions focused on making AI-assisted writing sound more human.

That gets us to a decent draft. Then the checks start.

They grade the article against standards I created. They look for unsupported claims, weak statements, and missing citations. They also catch repeated keywords, awkward phrasing, and questions the article never answered.

One of those checks works like a skeptical editor. Its whole job is to find problems.

It may tell me that a sentence needs a source. It may point out that I've used the same search phrase so many times that the article sounds forced. Then it shows me where I can remove that phrase without losing anything.

I revise the article, and it goes through the process again.

By the time I sit down to read it closely, it may have already been through several versions. My review is less mechanical.

I read the article as if I found it through Google and knew nothing about the work behind it.

Would I keep reading? Does this sound like a person wrote it? Is it easy to understand? Does it look exhausting before I even start? Can I find the answer I need?

Most of all, how does the article feel?

Once I'm happy with the research, writing, structure, and how the whole thing reads, I send the draft to Aaron. He checks it for accuracy and gives the final approval.

Only then does it go on the live website.
Some readers want to know everything. They want the law, the exceptions, the definitions, the process, the related questions, and the sources.

They don't want to visit four websites to piece together one complete answer.

I understand that person. Sometimes I am that person.

That's why some Magnolia articles take 10, 12, or even 16 minutes to read. I try to answer the next question someone might ask, and then the question after that, as long as it's still relevant.

The other reader is the person in the hospital hallway at 2 a.m.

They don't want a 16-minute reading assignment. They need to know what to do next.

I try to build each article for both people.

Every article starts with a short answer to the main question. If that's all someone needs, they can stop there. If they want more, the rest is waiting for them.

The articles also have a table of contents with links to each section. Paragraphs are short. Bullet points break up complicated information. Headings should tell readers exactly what they'll find underneath them.

The information can have depth without making someone carry all of it at once.

That structure also makes the page easier for search engines and AI tools to understand and quote correctly. The same choices that help a person who needs a fast answer can also help the technology find that answer.
The funeral industry has its own language. Most families don't use that language every day.

"Cremains" is a good example. It's an industry term for cremated remains. Most people just call them ashes, so that's usually the word we use.

If an article needs the official term for legal or technical accuracy, we'll use it. Then we'll explain that it means ashes.

We don't leave the reader to figure out the terminology alone.

We usually say someone "passed away" instead of saying they "died." Jargon comes out when it isn't needed. We also try to sound compassionate without becoming vague.

Some articles have to cover laws, official rules, or legal documents like death certificates. I can't soften those subjects so much that the information becomes inaccurate.

What I can do is explain them in normal language.

An article can cite a law without reading like a legal dissertation. It can also be thorough and accurate without making a grieving person feel like they need an attorney sitting next to them to understand it.

Keep the important complexity. Remove the unnecessary difficulty.
I can see the change in Google Search Console. That's Google's tool for showing [how often a website appears in search results and how often people click it](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/10268906).

From January through July 2025, Magnolia received 2,939 clicks from Google Search. During the same seven months in 2026, it received 11,107.

That's an increase of 278 percent.

The number of times Magnolia appeared in search results went from 655,056 to 3,943,190. That's an increase of 502 percent.

Blog articles received 5,402 clicks during the 2026 period. Those clicks account for 66 percent of all the clicks Magnolia added between the two periods.

The rest of the website grew too. Clicks to pages outside the blog went from 2,939 to 5,705, an increase of 94 percent.

I'm careful about what I claim from those numbers.

They show that Magnolia became far more visible in Google Search. They also show that the articles made up a big share of the added traffic.

They don't prove that the blog caused every part of the growth.

Magnolia's business has grown too. I believe the work we're doing across the website, the blog, Google Business Profile, and other places is helping more people find the company and choose it.

Believing that and proving it are two different things.

The search data can't tell me exactly why every family chose Magnolia. I'm not going to draw a straight line from a click chart to revenue and pretend that settles the question.

What I can say is that a lot more people are finding Magnolia online. The content system I built has played a major part in that.
I'm a big believer in teams. For most of my career, several people have had a hand in the final result. I like working that way.

But it also made it easy for me to downplay my own part.

I could always say, "That person contributed this. This person contributed that. It was a team effort."

All of that might be true. But it made it hard to point to something and say, "I did that, and it worked."

I can say that about this, although I still feel the need to add the qualifiers. So here they are.

Magnolia is a client of VYTL, the marketing agency where I work. Josh Koerber owns VYTL and is my boss. He already knew Magnolia needed a blog, and he gave me control of the content. The strategy and the day-to-day decisions were mine.

Aaron brings his professional knowledge, reviews the articles, and gives final approval. The rest of our team contributes to Magnolia's success in other ways.

But I built this content process.

I choose the subjects and shape the research. I developed the writing and review systems. I decide when an article is ready for Aaron.

Then I publish it, watch how it performs, and come back later to improve it.

For the first time in my career, I can look at real growth and say, "I had a big hand in that."

I'm proud of it.
AI was much less reliable when I started this work. The writing wasn't as good, and I had to question almost every part of the research.

I didn't know much about using Claude, either. I had mostly used ChatGPT to analyze spreadsheets, review reports, and bounce ideas around.

Two years later, the tools are dramatically better. I'm better at using them too.

I've turned what I learned into a group of Claude skills for research, writing, and review. "Skills" is simply Claude's name for reusable sets of instructions.

Those instructions now contain far more information about Magnolia, the industry, our standards, and the kind of article I'm trying to produce.

I could probably automate more of the process. I might even be able to set it up so I don't see an article until it's ready for Aaron.

I don't want to work that way.

I'm not the only person who could make these decisions. Josh could read an article and tell whether it feels overwhelming or easy to understand. So could any other thoughtful person.

I stay involved because I care about that part.

The tools help me research more, write faster, find gaps, and catch problems. I still want to read the article the way a reader would.

That's probably my sweet spot. I like combining the technology with the psychology.

I want to know what information a person needs. I also want to know how that information is going to make them feel.

I want the article to work for Google and the AI tools people use to search. I also want it to sound like one person trying to help another.
Search visibility is how that person finds the page.

Everything after that is for them.

Maybe they want to read every detail before making a decision. Maybe they only have enough energy for the first three sentences.

Either way, I want the page to meet them where they are.

When they finish reading, I hope they feel informed and capable of making the next decision.

I hope they feel relieved because at least one question has a clear answer.

I hope they feel understood.

Most of all, I hope they feel a little less alone.
